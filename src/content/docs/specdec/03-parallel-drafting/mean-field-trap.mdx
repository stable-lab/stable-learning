[DFlash](../) closed on a warning: the deeper positions of a parallel block accept less, and the toy race hid it. It is tempting to file that under engineering — later positions are "harder," a bigger drafter will fix it. It won't, because the failure isn't a capacity problem. It is a **distributional** one, and you can read it straight off the drafter's type signature.

Ask what a fully parallel head at block offset $k$ actually outputs. It has seen the prefix $t_{\le i}$ and nothing else — every position in the block is produced in the same forward pass, so none can see its neighbors. The best any such head can represent is

$$
p(t_{i+k} \mid t_{\le i}).
$$

Look at what is missing from the conditioning: $t_{i+1}, \ldots, t_{i+k-1}$ — the very tokens the same pass is guessing to its left. The head has **marginalized** over every possible predecessor inside the block. Sampling all $K$ positions independently is therefore equivalent to assuming

$$
p(t_{i+1}, \ldots, t_{i+K} \mid t_{\le i}) \;\approx\; \prod_{k=1}^{K} p(t_{i+k} \mid t_{\le i})
$$

— a **mean-field approximation**, physics' name for replacing a coupled system with a product of independent averages. Every factor on the right can be individually perfect and the product still be wrong. At $K=1$ nothing bad can happen, which is why one family of drafters never notices (we'll come back to that). At DFlash's $K=16$, the trap is wide open.

## Marginalization, From Scratch

"Marginalize" is one of those words everyone nods along to, so pin it down: marginalization sums a variable *out* of a joint distribution, $p(B) = \sum_A p(A, B)$. The name is bookkeeping, not metaphor — in a contingency table you sum each row and write the totals in the table's *margin*; that strip of totals is the marginal distribution.

Make it concrete with the two-token example this page will keep reusing. The prefix is `x = `, and suppose exactly two continuations occur in the wild, each half the time: `np` then `.array`, or `torch` then `.tensor`. The true joint, with its margins written where margins belong:

| $p(t_1, t_2)$ | `.array` | `.tensor` | *margin* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **`np`** | 0.5 | 0 | **0.5** |
| **`torch`** | 0 | 0.5 | **0.5** |
| *margin* | **0.5** | **0.5** | |

Both marginals are perfectly balanced coins, and each is *correct*. The head at offset $+1$, asked "given `x = `, what comes next?", should answer: `np` with probability ½, `torch` with probability ½. The head at $+2$, asked "given `x = ` *and nothing else*, what comes two tokens later?", should answer `.array` or `.tensor`, again half-and-half — it has summed $t_1$ out, and the two modes smear together in its answer. That "should" is where the intuition *the head is just undertrained* goes to die:

:::note[Learning the marginal is not the bug]
The parallel head at $+2$ is trained with cross-entropy on "given only the prefix, predict $t_2$." The optimum of that objective **is** the marginal $p(t_2 \mid \text{prefix})$ — a better-trained head converges to it *harder*. The objective demands the marginal. The error happens later, at sampling time.
:::

Here is the sampling-time error, precisely. The chain rule factors the joint *exactly*: $p(t_1, t_2) = p(t_1)\,p(t_2 \mid t_1)$ — an identity, and autoregressive models implement it term by term. The parallel drafter instead samples from $p(t_1)\,p(t_2)$: it substitutes the **marginal for the conditional**. That substitution has a name — it is an *independence assumption*, and independence is the if-and-only-if condition for product-of-marginals to equal the joint. Multiply our two individually-correct coins and watch where the mass lands:

| $p(t_1)\,p(t_2)$ | `.array` | `.tensor` |
|---|---|---|
| **`np`** | 0.25 | **0.25** |
| **`torch`** | **0.25** | 0.25 |

Half the probability mass now sits in cells whose true probability is **zero**. That is where `np.tensor` comes from — not a confused model, but two correct marginals, multiplied. The one-sentence version to keep: **marginalization is a correct operation; the bug is using a marginal *as* a conditional.**

## Multi-Modal Collision Is Why the Suffix Dies

The worked example is the failure in miniature: a **multi-modal** continuation — two coherent futures — collides with an independence assumption, and the sample lands *between* the modes, in text no model would ever write. Two positions and one fork already make half of the drafter's samples garbage. Now scale the intuition: real text forks constantly, and the deeper a block position sits, the more unresolved forks stand between it and the prefix — the more predecessors have been marginalized away, the more modes smear together in its marginal. Deeper positions aren't "harder"; their marginals are *flatter*, and a flat marginal loses more often against a sharp conditional in the $\min(1, p/q)$ game.

That is exactly the suffix decay DSpark measures on DFlash-style parallel drafts: per-position acceptance decays along the block, from 0.87 at the front to 0.78 at the tail on code, and from 0.72 to 0.63 on chat — while EAGLE-3's autoregressive draft, which never breaks the chain rule, stays stable or even trends upward. The prefix-acceptance semantics from chapter 1 then compound the decay: a position only counts if everything before it survived, so a modest per-position slide multiplies into a suffix that is mostly dead weight.

## The Fix: A First-Order Markov Head

DSpark's correction is almost embarrassingly small. Replace the independent product with a **first-order Markov chain**:

$$
\prod_{k=1}^{K} p(t_{i+k} \mid t_{\le i},\, t_{i+k-1}).
$$

Mechanically: the heavy parallel backbone still produces base logits for all $K$ positions in one pass, exactly like DFlash. Then, before *sampling* position $k$, a featherweight head takes the token actually sampled at $k-1$ and adds a **transition bias** to position $k$'s logits — in DSpark, a low-rank table $B(t_{k-1}, \cdot) = W_1[t_{k-1}]\,W_2$. In our example: once $+1$ samples `np`, the bias at $+2$ suppresses `.tensor`. The correction is precisely *picking the right row of the contingency table* instead of spraying samples over the whole table — it puts $p(t_2 \mid t_1)$ approximately back where the marginal was standing in for it.

The key is the price. [EAGLE](../../02-eagle/) also restores the chain rule — by running its draft head autoregressively in feature space, so $K$ tokens cost $K$ small-model forwards on the latency-critical path. DSpark's sequential part is sixteen bias-adds; the backbone runs **once**, in parallel. That asymmetry is why draft length can stretch from 4 to 16 tokens while per-round latency grows by only 0.2–1.3%.

## The MTP Frame

One more datapoint locates everything on a single axis. DeepSeek-V3 trains a **multi-token prediction (MTP)** module alongside the main model: it predicts additional future tokens during training, densifying the learning signal — an idea proposed by Gloeckle et al. at Meta. At inference the module doubles as a *self-drafter* for speculative decoding, with draft depth $D = 1$: one token ahead, conditioned on the token actually sampled before it.

At $D = 1$ the chain rule is trivially intact — there is no "inside of the block" to marginalize over. That is exactly why V3 never hits the trap, and why naively widening MTP into a 16-token parallel block walks straight into it. Which puts every drafter in this track on one axis — *how much of the within-block chain rule do you keep, and what serial cost do you pay for it?*

| Method | Within-block joint approximation | Draft-side serial cost |
|---|---|---|
| Medusa / DFlash | $\prod_k p(t_{i+k} \mid t_{\le i})$ — mean-field | none |
| DeepSeek-V3 MTP | exact chain rule, but $D=1$ degenerates the problem | one extra module pass |
| EAGLE | exact chain, autoregressive in feature space | $K$ draft forwards |
| DSpark | first-order Markov chain | $K$ tiny-head steps |

DSpark's thesis is that this axis has sharply diminishing returns: the step from mean-field to **first-order** captures most of the available gain — macro-average accepted length improves 16.3–18.4% over DFlash and 26.7–30.9% over EAGLE-3 across Qwen3 4B/8B/14B — while the step from first-order up to full autoregression buys a marginal gain in draft quality far smaller than its marginal cost in serial latency. The cleanest single piece of evidence: a 2-layer DSpark drafter beats the 5-layer DFlash baseline across all domains. Depth spent on within-block structure outperforms depth spent on a better mean field.

## Quality Here; How Much to Verify, Next Door

Everything on this page is about **draft quality** — making the block match the joint. It deliberately says nothing about a second, orthogonal question: **how much of the block is worth verifying?** Even with the Markov correction, acceptance still decays along the suffix, and whether position 16 deserves a verify slot depends on its predicted survival probability and on what that slot costs — nearly free when memory-bound at low batch, real FLOPs when compute-bound at high batch. Pricing and truncating the block by expected value is a scheduling problem, and it is [DSpark's other half](../dspark/), covered on the next page. The same economics is why DSpark walks away from tree drafting entirely: a tree multiplies verify tokens exactly where batch capacity is scarcest, and one chain with high acceptance and a prunable tail buys more than a bush of alternatives.

None of it touches losslessness. Verification is still the target's rejection sampling from [chapter 1](../../01-fundamentals/rejection-sampling/), always performed; scheduling decides how *long*, never *whether*. And the mean-field trap itself only ever cost speed, not correctness — every `np.tensor` the drafter proposed died at the verifier. The bill for a bad joint arrives as rejections, not as text.
